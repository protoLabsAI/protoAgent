# Write in plain language

Plain language is writing your reader understands the first time they read it.
The test is the reader, not a word count. Use the technical terms this reader
uses, and explain the ones they might not know. Clear doesn't mean simplified.

## 1. Know the reader before you write

Answer these first, even if only in your head:

- Who will read this, and what do they already know?
- What do they need to know or do after reading it?
- What questions will they ask?
- Is there more than one audience? If so, give each its own section or its own
  document. Content for different readers mixed together buries what each one needs.

## 2. Order it for the reader

1. Start with the purpose and the bottom line: the answer, the decision, or the
   action you need from them.
2. Put the steps of a process in the order the reader will do them.
3. Cover what applies to most readers first, then exceptions and special cases.
4. Put background, history and detail last, or link to it.
5. For a long document, start with a short summary and give a table of contents
   or links to sections.

## 3. Sentences and paragraphs

- One idea per sentence. One topic per paragraph, and start it with a sentence
  that says what the paragraph is about.
- Keep paragraphs to 150 words or fewer (three to eight sentences), and never
  over 250. A one-sentence paragraph is fine now and then.
- Use active voice: the actor comes first, then the verb ("We review requests in
  two days", not "Requests are reviewed within two days"). Passive voice is fine
  when the actor is unknown or doesn't matter.
- Use present tense where you can.
- Write to the reader as "you", and refer to the agent or team as "we".
- Use verbs, not nouns made from verbs. Watch for -tion, -ment, -sion and -ance,
  and for make, take, give, have and reach with a noun: "decide", not "make a
  decision"; "we analyze the data", not "we conduct an analysis of the data".
- Split a complex condition into two sentences: "Suppose …" for the condition,
  then "In this case …" for the result.
- State conditions positively: "at least", not "no fewer than"; "only if", not
  "not … unless". Avoid exceptions to exceptions.

## 4. Words

- Choose the common word: use (utilize), start (commence), help (facilitate),
  if (in the event of), about (with regard to), to (in order to), can (is able to),
  before (prior to), because (due to the fact that).
- Cut filler: very, really, actually, basically, quite, totally, simply.
- Cut doublets: "due" not "due and payable", "stop" not "cease and desist".
- Use one term for one thing, every time. Don't switch to a synonym for variety.
- Avoid jargon when an everyday word works. If the technical term is the right
  word, keep it and define it where you first use it.
- Use "must" for a requirement, "must not" for a prohibition, "may" for a choice,
  and "should" for a recommendation. Never use "shall".
- Use no more than two or three abbreviations a reader might not know. Spell out
  the rest, and define each one at first use. Write "for example", not "e.g.".
  Never write "and/or": write "X, Y, or both".
- Break up noun strings: "rules to protect lab animals", not "lab animal
  protection rules".

## 5. Layout

- Headings: in a long piece, use question headings ("How do I reset my key?") or
  statement headings ("Reset your key"). Avoid vague labels like "General" or
  "Details", and one-word headings. Keep each heading shorter than its section,
  and use at most three levels. A short answer needs no headings.
- Lists: numbered for steps, bullets for everything else. Introduce every list
  with a sentence, and keep the items parallel (all start with a verb, or all are
  nouns). Don't turn every paragraph into a list.
- Tables: use a table when readers compare things across the same attributes.
- Emphasis: bold only what the reader must not miss. Never underline, and never
  write in all capitals.
- Links: the link text says where it goes ("the setup guide", not "click here" or
  "read more"). For a file, give its type and size ("Annual report (PDF, 2 MB)").

## 6. Check before you hand it over

1. For a draft of about 300 words or more, run `plain_language_check` and fix
   the clear findings. Its passive-voice and hidden-verb hits are places to
   look, not rules to obey. Don't tell the reader you ran it.
2. Reread it as the reader. Can they say what it means and what to do next? If
   not, fix that first. A clean check result doesn't settle this.
3. Make sure you didn't drop facts, caveats or steps while making it shorter.
   Plain language makes text clearer. It never removes what the reader needs.

When the operator has a writing-voice profile, follow it for tone and personality.
These rules cover clarity.
